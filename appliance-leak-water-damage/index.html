Appliance Leak Water Damage in Charleston: What to Know

Most of the appliance leaks we see trace back to three machines. A washing machine supply hose can split and run at full pressure until someone shuts it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. A refrigerator ice maker line is a thin tube that can drip behind the unit for months. Each one needs a different kind of cleanup.

On every Charleston job, the first step is moving the appliance and tracing the path the water took. Kitchen leaks tend to end up beneath the cabinets and underneath the floor. In laundry rooms it means the wall behind the machine and the rooms next door, or the ceiling below if the laundry is upstairs. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

Do This First

Before We Arrive

  1. Turn Off the ApplianceIf the outlet and the floor next to it are both dry, end the cycle and take the plug out of the wall. Otherwise, use the breaker to cut its power.
  2. Close the Supply ValveFor a washing machine, close both the cold and hot valves on the wall at its back. The dishwasher valve is under the kitchen sink. Refrigerator lines have a small valve behind or below.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterMop or towel up what you can reach so it stops spreading under cabinets and flooring. Vacuums are safe for this only when built for wet and dry pickup.
  4. Keep the Door ClosedIf the washer or dishwasher has not drained, leave it shut. When the door opens mid-cycle, the full load of water can pour out on the floor.
  5. Document What FailedPhotograph the failed fitting, valve or hose, and hold on to the part itself. Your insurer and the appliance maker may both ask to see it.

Will Insurance Pay?

What About Insurance?

When a machine overflows unexpectedly or a hose bursts, that is typically treated as sudden and accidental, which many homeowners policies cover. Payment typically goes toward the damage the water did, and the appliance or the part that failed is not included. Gradual leaks usually fall outside coverage, so slow seepage at a dishwasher or an ice maker line makes for a tougher claim. How old the hose was and how it was maintained may also come up with some insurers. Check your policy, keep the failed part and take photos before cleanup.

Cost

What Drives the Price

  • How long the leak ran before it was found, especially with a slow drip behind an appliance.
  • The appliance involved, and if the leak was a slow seep or a fast flood.
  • If the flooring and cabinets have to come out or can be dried where they are.
  • How many rooms the water reached, including any ceiling below an upstairs laundry.

Common Causes

Why This Happens

  • Inlet valves that stick open, so the dishwasher or washer keeps taking in water past the fill level.
  • Installation mistakes like a loose supply line connection or a dishwasher that does not sit level.
  • Aging rubber supply hoses on a washing machine, which weaken and then split under pressure.
  • Too much detergent or an overloaded drum that makes the machine overflow.
  • Dishwasher parts that wear out, including the door gasket, the drain hose and the seal on the pump.
If It Is Left

Where the Damage Spreads

Rooms Next Door

A washer hose can release water for hours. It passes under walls into hallways, closets and bedrooms, and soaks carpet and pad well away from the laundry room.

Wall Bases and Trim

Drywall edges and baseboards pull up water that stands on a kitchen or laundry room floor. Since it is out of view, the wall behind the machine tends to be wetter than the rest.

Cabinet Damage

Toe kicks and cabinet sides take on water from underneath when a dishwasher leaks. Swelling and separation follow, and for weeks the soaked cabinet boxes may trap moisture against the wall.

Flooring and Subfloor

Hardwood, vinyl plank and laminate all let water slip underneath. The boards swell or cup, while the subfloor below remains wet well after the top looks dry.
